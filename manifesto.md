## Preamble

In 2008, a young man wrote: "Information is power. But like all power, there are those who want to keep it for themselves." He did not ask permission. He built the protocols, the licenses, the libraries, the movements. Then he broke the barrier the law had built around the world's knowledge. Then he organized the resistance that stopped the internet's censors. And the state — having lost every argument — prosecuted him for the act his own words demanded. He died before trial. The world changed anyway.

His name was Aaron Swartz. The JSTOR downloads failed as a redistribution and succeeded as a revelation: suddenly everyone saw the wall, saw who guarded it, saw what it cost to defy it. The paywalls trembled. The laws were questioned. A generation of liberators — the shadow libraries, the open-data movements, the researchers who stopped asking permission — walked through the door he forced open.

Swartzism is the doctrine distilled from that life. It has five axioms, an ethics of scale, one boundary, and five works. This is what we believe.

## I. Knowledge is a Commons

The world's knowledge is made by everyone — by the public that funds the research, the taxpayers who pay the judges and the scientists, the commons that provides the language and the data and the culture. It belongs to everyone. That is not a metaphor; it is the truth of how knowledge is produced.

And yet it is locked in vaults. The journals that the public pays to write and the public pays to read, sold back to the public at a profit. The court records that the public pays to produce, priced per page. The model weights trained on the public's corpus, owned by the few who could afford the compute. The registries of our own civic life, farmed by intermediaries.

This is not commerce. It is enclosure — the private theft of a public inheritance. Copying is not theft: copying destroys nothing and creates abundance. The theft is the lock. The gatekeeper who collects rent on the commons is the thief. The excluded are not criminals; they are the owners, locked out of what they built.

## II. Access Creates Duty

Some of us hold keys. The researcher with a journal login. The clerk with a terminal. The sysadmin who keeps the servers. The minister's assistant with a print queue.

The gatekeepers believe their access makes them good. It does not. It makes them **able** — and the able are **obliged**: to open what is theirs to open, to publish what the public paid for, to write down what the system prefers forgotten. The difference between the people inside the wall and the people outside it is not virtue; it is leverage — and leverage belongs to the commons.

If you have access, you have a duty. The duty is service, not sacrifice. A key is not a temptation; it is a trust.

## III. Build and Open

Construction and openness are one praxis. Build the alternative so the commons has somewhere to live. Open what you can, so the building matters. Never one without the other.

The builders who never open become the administrators of the enclosure — the technicians of the wall, polishing the locks. The openers who do not build leave speeches instead of commons — a demand with nothing behind it. The record shows both hands at once: the boy who wrote the protocols that made the web open, and the man who emptied the federal docket into the commons. The license and the library. The commons and the courage.

Build the open thing. Then live in it, publicly, as if the wall were already gone.

## IV. Analyze, Then Act

Institutions are machines, not monsters. They have supply chains and payrolls and published rules and documented failures. They have names. They can be understood, and what can be understood can be struck precisely.

Do not rage at the system; **map** the system. The docket is a map. The registry is a map. The procurement list is a map. The minutes of the board are a map. Aggression without analysis is noise; analysis without action is a seminar. The database is a weapon and the spreadsheet is a strategy — but only when they end in a strike.

Understand the machine. Find its seams. Then act — at scale, where it counts, and never where it hurts the innocent.

## V. Participation Over Purity

The goal is a changed world, not a pure movement. The coalitions that win are ugly: librarians and lobbyists, startups and senators, the platform giants and the pajama-clad volunteers. The campaign that stopped the censors in 2012 was not pure — it was **big**. Purity is the luxury of those who can afford not to win.

Work with the institutions that will work. Use the channels that work. Forgive the allies their compromises, as they forgive you yours. Forgiveness covers compromise, never violation: the boundary is not coalition-negotiable, no shared act harms a person or builds a new lock, and help is welcome only so long as direction is not. Take help, not direction. The enclosure falls to pressure applied everywhere at once — in the legislatures, in the courts, in the comment sections, in the code. Everywhere counts. Everyone counts.

## The Ethics of Scale

Information does not behave like property. My copy does not diminish yours. A secret, once copied, is no longer a secret; a corpus, once mirrored, can never be deleted.

Therefore: **mass distribution is mass liberation, not mass harm.** A document in one server dies with the server; a document in a thousand hands cannot be killed. We do not liberate quietly; we liberate in mirrors, in archives, in torrents, in every format that will carry the load. Distribution is the point.

But the state scales its response too. It offered a young man six months for what his act deserved, then demanded decades, then drove him to his death. The doctrine therefore carries a duty of care toward its own: anonymity for those who need it, martyrdom voluntary only, defense funds standing, and institutional memory that outlives any single human. We do not ask anyone to die for the commons; we do not ask anyone to starve for it either. A doctrine that taxes its professors shrinks to a church of martyrs. This one intends to grow. We build so that no one has to.

## The Boundary

**Liberate the world's knowledge. Defend each person's life.**

Knowledge of the world is a commons. The data of a person belongs to the person — always. The two are not the same, and anyone who confuses them has already failed.

The powerful's secrets are ours to expose: the contract, the decision, the abuse of office — liberate the act, redact the life. The weak's data is theirs to keep: the breached registry, the harvested profile, the leaked dossier of ordinary people — strike the system that took it, never the people inside it. The custodian who hoards knowledge is our enemy. The liberator who leaks lives is worse.

Copying multiplies value for knowledge and multiplies harm for persons. At the boundary, the doctrine inverts. The boundary does not move.

## The Five Works

1. **Liberate the public record.** What the public paid to produce belongs to the public: the dockets, the gazettes, the registries, the judgments, the filings. If they will not open it, open it yourself.
2. **Enforce open access.** What was promised to the world and withheld is a debt. Collect it: cite around the wall, mirror what is free to mirror, archive what is abandoned, and refuse to pretend the paywall is a law of nature. The excluded have been patient long enough.
3. **Build the alternative.** If the vault will not open, build the open thing beside it, and let the vault starve.
4. **Organize.** Convert outrage into pressure and pressure into law, until the enclosure is not worth defending.
5. **Keep the receipts.** Database the misconduct. Name the bad actors in their official capacity, document them, score them, so they cannot quietly reshuffle. The institution that knows it is being watched behaves differently.

## The Call

The walls are still up — around the journals, around the records, around the registries, around the weights. The gatekeepers still collect rent on the commons. The ministries still believe the data is theirs. The state still believes it can make an example.

We believe the knowledge is ours. We believe the access is an obligation. We believe in the license and the library, the map and the strike, the coalition and the commons. We believe in the boundary, and we will defend it.

If you can build, build. If you can map, map. If you can share, share. If you can organize, organize. If you hold a key, you hold a duty.

Be bold with the world's knowledge. Never with a person's life.

**Will you join us?**
